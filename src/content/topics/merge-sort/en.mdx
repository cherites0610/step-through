## What merge sort does

Merge sort is divide and conquer: split a big problem into small ones, solve them, and combine the answers.

1. **Split**: cut the range in the middle into a left and a right half and recurse on each until a single number is left. A single number is always sorted.
2. **Merge**: combine two sorted halves into one. Repeatedly compare the front numbers of the two halves and take the smaller into the result; when one half runs out, append what is left of the other.
3. Go back up a level and keep merging until the whole array is merged.

## How to read the picture

- **The recursion tree on top**: one row per level, with columns aligned to the indices of the original array. A dashed box is a range that has been split, a pale blue box is a range that is sorted, and the heavy box is the range being handled now.
- **The merge area below**: three rows - left half, right half and merged output. Numbers already taken are struck through, the one just taken is solid, and the two front numbers are boxed heavily: they are what this step compares.
- **The call stack on the right**: recursive calls that have not returned yet, with the current one on top. It is only ⌈log₂ n⌉ + 1 deep, so it does not grow quickly with the data.

## Why O(n log n)

Seven numbers reach single elements after 3 levels of halving, so the number of levels is log n. At each level, merging compares and writes each number at most once, n in total. Levels times work per level is n log n.

Try "Sorted" and "Random": the number of comparisons differs a little, but the number of writes is almost the same, because every number is written once per level.
